\title{\mfabric{}: A Post-Quantum Cryptographic Multilayer Architecture for Hyperledger Fabric}

\author{Zara Laila Cheong\authorrefmark{1}, Ali Tufail\authorrefmark{1}, and Rosyzie Anna Apong\authorrefmark{1}}
\affil{School of Digital Science, Universiti Brunei Darussalam, Bandar Seri Begawan, BE1410, Brunei Darussalam}
\corresp{Corresponding author: Zara Laila Cheong (email: zaralaila@icloud.com).}
%\authornote{The authors declare no specific funding for this work and no conflicts of interest. The implementation, benchmark scripts, and measurement data are openly available in the M\textsuperscript{2}Fabric repository to support independent reproduction.}


\begin{abstract}
{Hyperledger Fabric relies on classical elliptic-curve cryptography across
its entire transaction lifecycle, leaving every signing, certification,
validation, and key-exchange operation vulnerable to a cryptographically
relevant quantum computer. Prior post-quantum proposals each replaced only
a subset of Fabric's cryptographic layers, leaving the remainder
quantum-exploitable. This article presents \mfabric{}, a multilayer
post-quantum architecture that simultaneously replaces all four live
cryptographic layers of Hyperledger Fabric v2.5.12 and Fabric CA v1.5.12:
the Blockchain Cryptographic Service Provider (BCCSP) signing layer, the
certificate infrastructure, the Membership Service Provider
identity-validation layer, and the Transport Layer Security (TLS)
inter-node transport layer. The design uses NIST-standardised
ML-DSA-44/65 (FIPS~204), FN-DSA-512 (FIPS~206 Initial Public Draft), and
ML-KEM-768 (FIPS~203) through pure-Go libraries and is deployed in three
migration phases. The principal engineering contribution is
\textit{buildPQCChain()}, a certificate-chain validator that bypasses the
Go standard library's rejection of post-quantum object identifiers,
enabling a live certificate authority to issue and validate post-quantum
certificates at runtime. Verification spans NIST ACVP coverage,
interface-compliance, round-trip, and end-to-end transaction execution
with zero failures. An empirical evaluation on a two-organisation Caliper
network ($n=27$ runs per workload plus a six-run ECDSA baseline) shows the
Phase~3 deployment reduces maximum sustainable write-heavy throughput
from the 275.3~TPS ECDSA baseline by 3.5\% for FN-DSA-512, 10.9\% for
ML-DSA-44, and 18.0\% for ML-DSA-65, with bootstrap and Cohen's-$d$
checks supporting the ordering. The results establish per-endorsement
payload size, not signing speed, as the dominant throughput determinant.}
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION} \label{sec:intro}
%=====================================================================
\IEEEPARstart{H}{yperledger} Fabric's quantum vulnerability is complete
and uniform across the twenty-five cryptographic operations of its
transaction lifecycle and identity-management infrastructure. Replacing
all four live cryptographic layers — the BCCSP signing layer, the X.509
certificate-infrastructure layer, the MSP identity-validation layer, and
the TLS inter-node communication layer — with NIST-standardised
post-quantum equivalents while preserving compatibility with the Fabric
component ecosystem is the central engineering challenge. Prior work has
addressed subsets in
isolation~\cite{holcomb2021, hu2025prototype, abbas2025, kasula2025, razdan2025postquantum},
but no published implementation addresses all four simultaneously, and
partial integration creates a structural vulnerability in which the
unaddressed layers remain quantum-exploitable regardless of the
post-quantum strength of the addressed layers.

The implementation presented here, \mfabric{}, provides a post-quantum
BCCSP plugin supporting ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65, and FN-DSA-512 through
constant-time pure-Go libraries; extends the live Fabric CA to issue
X.509 certificates carrying post-quantum public keys; implements a
custom OID-aware certificate-chain validator in the MSP without
modifying the Go standard library; and activates ML-KEM-768 hybrid key
exchange at the TLS layer. The contributions beyond existing literature
are: (i) the first runtime implementation addressing the four primary
cryptographic layers of Fabric v2.5.x simultaneously under
FIPS-standardised algorithms (FIPS~203, 204, FIPS~206 Initial Public
Draft) in unmodified Go; (ii) the \textit{buildPQCChain()} OID-dispatched
validator that solves the Go standard-library X.509 barrier that has
prevented live CA integration in prior
work~\cite{holcomb2021, hu2025prototype, abbas2025}; (iii) the first
end-to-end performance characterisation of a four-layer FIPS-standardised
integration on Fabric v2.5.12 ($n=27$ benchmark runs per workload across
nine algorithm-phase combinations plus a six-run ECDSA baseline),
establishing per-endorsement payload size as the dominant throughput
determinant; and (iv) resolution of practical engineering barriers
(certificate-generation and chain-validation bypasses for post-quantum
OIDs, variable-length FN-DSA signature handling, and CFSSL hook-based
integration).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews the existing literature.
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the methodology. Section~\ref{sec:experimental} describes the
experimental setup, test network, verification procedure, and benchmark
methodology, and reports the verification and performance results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses completeness,
generalisability, alignment with national PQC migration strategies, and
limitations, while Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{LITERATURE REVIEW} \label{sec:litreview}
%=====================================================================

This section reviews post-quantum standardisation, post-quantum TLS
performance, and prior Fabric integrations. The recurring gap is that
prior Fabric work has each replaced only a strict subset of the
platform's cryptographic layers, leaving the unaddressed layers
quantum-exploitable irrespective of the strength of those replaced.

\subsection{POST-QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY AND STANDARDISATION}

Shor's algorithm renders the elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm problem
and integer factorisation tractable, breaking ECDSA, ECDH, and RSA. NIST's
six-year standardisation~\cite{alagic2022nist} produced FIPS~203
(ML-KEM)~\cite{nist_fips203}, FIPS~204
(ML-DSA)~\cite{nist_fips204}, FIPS~205 (SLH-DSA)~\cite{nist_fips205}, and
the FIPS~206 Initial Public Draft (FN-DSA)~\cite{nist_fips206}. ML-DSA
derives from CRYSTALS-Dilithium~\cite{ducas2018dilithium} and FN-DSA from
Falcon~\cite{pornin2019, prest2020falcon}. NIST guidance recommends
hybrid constructions for transitional security~\cite{barker2020transition,
bindel2017}, instantiated for TLS by the IETF hybrid
framework~\cite{ietf_hybrid_tls} and the \textit{X25519MLKEM768}
codepoint~\cite{ietf_x25519mlkem768}. Ravi et
al.~\cite{ravi2024sidechannel} catalogue side-channel attacks against
lattice schemes and confirm constant-time arithmetic is necessary but not
sufficient for adversarial deployment~\cite{pessl2019}.

\subsection{POST-QUANTUM TLS}

Paquin et al.~\cite{paquin2020benchmark} and Sikeridis et
al.~\cite{sikeridis2020} characterised post-quantum TLS and found the
principal cost is payload (larger KEM ciphertexts and signatures) rather
than per-operation computation, a theme that recurs at the application
layer in the present work. The Open Quantum Safe
\textit{liboqs}~\cite{stebila2016oqs} and Cloudflare CIRCL provide the
reference implementations; CIRCL's pure-Go ML-DSA and ML-KEM run in
production at Cloudflare scale.

\subsection{POST-QUANTUM HYPERLEDGER FABRIC}

Hyperledger Fabric~\cite{androulaki2018} is a permissioned blockchain whose
transaction throughput under classical cryptography has been characterised by
Baliga et al.~\cite{baliga2018performance}, Kuzlu et
al.~\cite{kuzlu2021performance}, Nasir et al.~\cite{nasir2018}, and Thakkar et
al.~\cite{thakkar2018}, whose analyses identify endorsement-signature
verification at the committing peer as a principal scaling bottleneck. 

Prior post-quantum work on Hyperledger Fabric falls into four categories.
\emph{Algorithm benchmarks without Fabric integration}
(Campbell~\cite{campbell2019, campbell2019eval}; qTESLA was eliminated
from NIST Round~3~\cite{alagic2022nist}).
\emph{Attack-surface surveys} (Brotsis et al.~\cite{brotsis2020}).
\emph{Application-layer integrations} place primitives in chaincode or
enclaves while leaving Fabric's BCCSP/CA/MSP/TLS classical:
Lee~\cite{lee2025} (ML-DSA, Vesper, TDUE under Intel SGX), Das et
al.~\cite{das2025genomic} (Kyber/Dilithium in genomic chaincode), and
Revathi and Suganthi~\cite{revathi2025} (Kyber/Falcon/Dilithium in
hospital chaincode). \emph{Endorsement-policy proxies} configure
multi-organisation signatures as governance rather than cryptographic
replacement: Razdan and Nene~\cite{razdan2025postquantum} report 119\%
latency increase under dual-signature endorsement, with no cryptographic
modification.

The four \emph{infrastructure-layer integrations}
(Kasula et al.~\cite{kasula2025}, PQFabric (Holcomb et
al.)~\cite{holcomb2021}, Abbas et al.~\cite{abbas2025}, and
Hu~\cite{hu2025prototype}) each address a strict subset of the four
cryptographic layers. PQFabric used CGo bindings to \textit{liboqs} with
pre-FIPS parameters and established that per-payload overhead, not
signing speed, dominates performance — an observation \mfabric{} extends
to FIPS-finalised algorithms. Abbas et al.\ added post-quantum
certificate generation to the offline Cryptogen tool but did not address
the live CA, TLS, or MSP chain validation; Hu targeted Fabric~3.0 but
required a non-standard Go compiler fork.

The common limitation across all four infrastructure-layer integrations
is the incompatibility between post-quantum OIDs and Go's
\textit{crypto/x509}, which confined prior work to offline certificate
generation and left the live CA's classical signing key active.
\mfabric{} resolves this barrier and is, to the authors' knowledge, the
first integration to address all four live cryptographic layers of the
current Fabric LTS simultaneously using FIPS-standardised algorithms in
unmodified Go. Table~\ref{tab:prior_work_comparison} consolidates the
coverage, with checkmarks for runtime layers, ``offline'' for
offline-only addressing, ``app.'' for application-layer-only primitives,
and ``proxy'' for endorsement-policy mechanisms rather than
cryptographic replacements. PQFabric and Hu use pre-FIPS lattice
parameters; Kasula et al.'s primary SM2 algorithm is elliptic-curve-based
and is therefore equally vulnerable to Shor's algorithm despite the
``post-quantum'' framing.

\section{METHODOLOGY} \label{sec:methodology}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{CONSTRUCTION METHODOLOGY}
\label{sec:method_construction}

The implementation follows a design-science artefact-construction
methodology. The target platform is Hyperledger Fabric v2.5.12 (commit
\textit{af0b647}) and Fabric CA v1.5.12. Cloudflare CIRCL v1.6.3
provides pure-Go ML-DSA-44/65 and ML-KEM-768; \textit{go-fn-dsa} v0.2.0
provides FN-DSA-512. Go~1.24 is the minimum required version (it
introduced \textit{tls.X25519MLKEM768}, replacing the pre-standard
\textit{X25519Kyber768Draft00} from Go~1.23). The implementation is
structured as non-invasive patches that preserve existing module
structure, interface contracts, and test coverage by introducing
post-quantum functionality through new files and types rather than
modifying existing functions wherever feasible.

\subsection{DESIGN REQUIREMENTS AND PRINCIPLES}
\label{sec:method_principles}

The implementation is governed by the eight formal integration
requirements (R1--R8) derived from the companion quantum threat
analysis for Hyperledger Fabric~\cite{cheong2026threat}, which
enumerates twenty-five quantum-vulnerable lifecycle operations and
consolidates them into five threat scenarios: identity impersonation
via certificate forgery (TS1), transaction-signature forgery (TS2),
block-integrity compromise via orderer key recovery (TS3), TLS session
decryption via harvest-now-decrypt-later (TS4), and gossip-protocol
manipulation (TS5). Table~\ref{tab:req_traceability} lists the eight
requirements, their targets, and their category (functional,
architectural, or quality/security/operational). R1--R3 are functional
requirements that map directly to the threat scenarios; R4--R8 are
non-functional requirements that a deployable, maintainable,
side-channel-resistant, and incrementally deployable implementation
must satisfy.

The most technically demanding requirement is R3: it requires
bypassing Go's standard-library X.509 certificate-generation API for
post-quantum keys, making the \textit{buildPQCChain()} validator
(\S\ref{sec:method_msp}) a direct consequence, since a live CA that
issues post-quantum certificates requires a live validator that can
verify them.

The multilayer coverage objective can be stated formally. Let
$\mathcal{L}=\{\ell_1,\dots,\ell_4\}$ be the set of quantum-vulnerable
cryptographic layers and let $x_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether layer
$\ell$ is migrated to a post-quantum primitive. The requirements jointly
demand the coverage constraint

\begin{equation}
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}} x_{\ell} = |\mathcal{L}| = 4 ,
\label{eq:coverage_constraint}
\end{equation}

where $x_{\ell}$ is the binary migration indicator for layer $\ell$ and
$|\mathcal{L}|$ is the number of vulnerable layers. Any solution with
$\sum_{\ell} x_{\ell}<4$ leaves at least one layer quantum-exploitable; the
multilayer design exists precisely to satisfy~(\ref{eq:coverage_constraint})
with equality, distinguishing \mfabric{} from the partial integrations of prior
work.

\subsection{POST-QUANTUM ALGORITHM SELECTION}
\label{sec:method_alg}

Having fixed the design principles, the next step is to select the concrete
algorithms that instantiate them. The selection is constrained by R1 to
NIST-standardised algorithms from FIPS~203, 204, 205, or 206, and is then guided
by four criteria: security-level appropriateness, anticipated blockchain
performance, availability of production-quality pure-Go implementations, and
compatibility with Go's certificate and TLS infrastructure.

\subsubsection{SELECTION COST MODEL}

For a candidate signature algorithm $a$, define the deployment cost

\begin{equation}
C(a) \;=\; w_{p}\,\Pi(a) \,+\, w_{s}/\kappa(a) \,-\, w_{c}\,\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Go}}(a),
\label{eq:selection_cost}
\end{equation}

where $\Pi(a)=|\sigma_a|+|\mathit{cert}_a|$ is the per-endorsement payload
(signature plus DER-encoded X.509 certificate),
$\kappa(a)\in\{1,2,3,5\}$ is the NIST security category,
$\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Go}}(a)$ is unity when a production pure-Go
implementation exists, and $w_p, w_s, w_c\ge 0$ are deployment-specific
weights. The blockchain context drives $w_p$ high because $\Pi(a)$ is
paid on every endorsement in every block while signing latency is
amortised; this is the formal reason that signature size, not signing
speed, dominates selection, as the empirical results
(\S\ref{sec:res_consolidated}) confirm.

\subsubsection{DIGITAL SIGNATURE ALGORITHM SELECTION}

Three signature algorithms are evaluated. \textbf{ML-DSA} (FIPS~204) is
based on module-lattice Module-LWE~\cite{ducas2018dilithium}: ML-DSA-44
provides NIST Category~2 (2{,}420-byte signatures) and ML-DSA-65
provides Category~3 (3{,}309 bytes), both deterministic and
$33.6\times$--$46.0\times$ larger than ECDSA P-256 (71--72~bytes).
\textbf{FN-DSA-512} (FIPS~206 IPD) is based on NTRU/SIS
lattices~\cite{pornin2019, prest2020falcon} and provides Category~1
security with a compact variable-length Gaussian signature averaging
$\approx 652$~bytes (666-byte padded, 809-byte maximum), roughly
$3.6\times$ smaller than ML-DSA-44; the trade-off is signing
$\approx 53\%$ slower than ML-DSA-44 in microbenchmarks — a relationship
that inverts when output size dominates, as in the blockchain context.
\textbf{SLH-DSA} (FIPS~205)~\cite{nist_fips205} offers hash-based
algorithmic diversity but its 7{,}856--49{,}856-byte signatures would
inflate ledger storage by $100$--$700\times$ over ECDSA and is excluded
from the primary integration. Table~\ref{tab:alg_comparison}
consolidates the size parameters.

The certificate (DER-encoded) carries three algorithm-scaling
components: the holder's SubjectPublicKeyInfo, the issuing CA's
signature over the TBS structure, and a fixed $\sim$440~byte
RFC~5280~\cite{rfc5280} envelope. Measured cert sizes (\mfabric{}
Fabric CA, Fabric-realistic DNs) are 570, 1{,}945, 4{,}126, and 5{,}655
bytes for ECDSA P-256, FN-DSA-512, ML-DSA-44, and ML-DSA-65
respectively. Figure~\ref{fig:endorsement_overhead} translates these
sizes into the per-endorsement payload Fabric commits to the ledger. The combined
overhead $\Pi(a) = |\sigma_a| + |\mathit{cert}_a|$ is the sum of the
endorser's signature and the endorser's serialised identity certificate;
both are transmitted in every endorsement and recorded on every block.
For a single-endorser policy and a 500-transaction block, every block
carries $500\,\Pi(a)$ of additional payload over the chaincode-level
read-write sets. With a post-quantum CA, the certificate's issuer
signature uses the same algorithm as the endorser's signature, so a
single endorsement effectively pays the cost of two post-quantum
signatures plus one post-quantum public key plus the fixed envelope.
Measured values are $\Pi=641$~bytes for ECDSA P-256 (71-byte signature
plus 570-byte certificate), $2{,}611$~bytes for FN-DSA-512
($4.07\times$), $6{,}546$~bytes for ML-DSA-44 ($10.21\times$), and
$8{,}964$~bytes for ML-DSA-65 ($13.98\times$); the additional overhead
each post-quantum algorithm imposes over the ECDSA baseline is therefore
$1{,}970$, $5{,}905$, and $8{,}323$~bytes per endorsement, respectively,
and Figure~\ref{fig:endorsement_overhead} decomposes each bar into the
shared 641-byte baseline and this excess. The Falcon-derived
FN-DSA-512 keeps both the signature ($\sim$666~bytes average) and the
public key (897~bytes) compact relative to the ML-DSA family; the
Dilithium-derived ML-DSA family pays for its faster signing and constant
verification time with larger output sizes, and the Category-3 variant
ML-DSA-65 adds approximately 37~percent over ML-DSA-44. These multipliers
predict the relative ordering observed in \S\ref{sec:res_consolidated}
and confirm that certificate-and-signature size, not signing or
verification time, is the primary throughput determinant.

Microbenchmarks confirm the trade-off: ML-DSA-44 signs in
$\approx458$~\textmu s and FN-DSA-512 in $\approx698$~\textmu s (53\%
slower), with similar verification times — but FN-DSA-512's
$3.6\times$-smaller signature reverses the ranking when block size
dominates. ML-DSA-65 is retained for Category-3-mandated deployments at
a $\approx37\%$ signature-size premium over ML-DSA-44.

\subsubsection{KEY ENCAPSULATION MECHANISM SELECTION}

For TLS key exchange, ML-KEM-768 (FIPS~203, NIST Category~3) is
selected~\cite{nist_fips203}. Go's \textit{tls.X25519MLKEM768}
named-curve constant combines X25519 classical ECDH with ML-KEM-768
without additional dependencies and is standardised by the IETF as a
TLS~1.3 key-exchange
group~\cite{ietf_hybrid_tls}. The choice of Category~3 for ML-KEM relative to
Category~1 for FN-DSA-512 reflects asymmetric performance sensitivity: KEM is
performed once per TLS session, so the higher security level is negligible
relative to the per-transaction signature cost.

\subsection{MULTILAYER ARCHITECTURE}
\label{sec:method_arch}

Figure~\ref{fig:m2fabric_architecture} presents the \mfabric{}
four-layer architecture (purple: BCCSP signing; teal: CA/MSP identity;
blue: TLS transport), showing for each layer its primary integration
point, the library and standard it depends on, the phase in which it
activates, and the threat scenarios it closes.

\begin{figure*}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_m2fabric_architecture.png}
\caption{Multilayer post-quantum architecture.}
\label{fig:m2fabric_architecture}
\end{figure*}

The BCCSP layer implements Fabric's \textit{bccsp.Key}/\textit{bccsp.KeyGen}
interfaces through a pure-Go PQC plugin (CIRCL for ML-DSA,
\textit{go-fn-dsa} for FN-DSA, no CGo). The Fabric CA layer implements
the CFSSL \textit{crypto.Signer} interface and constructs post-quantum
X.509 certificates via \textit{CreatePQCCertificate()}, bypassing
\textit{x509.CreateCertificate} through custom ASN.1 encoding. The MSP
layer implements \textit{buildPQCChain()} in \textit{msp/pqc.go},
dispatching chain validation on the child's \textit{signatureAlgorithm}
OID and enforcing validity-period, \textit{keyCertSign}, and AKID/SKID
checks, bypassing \textit{x509.Certificate.Verify}. The TLS layer sets
\textit{tls.X25519MLKEM768} as the preferred key-agreement identifier.
Each layer is a self-contained set of source changes interacting with
the others only through existing Fabric interface contracts: the
certificate format produced by the CA is compatible with the MSP chain
validator, embedded public keys are importable by the BCCSP plugin, and
algorithm OIDs in the detection table match the certificate generator's.

\subsubsection{THREE-PHASE MIGRATION DESIGN}

The implementation is structured in three phases that progressively activate the
four layers, satisfying R8 and enabling per-layer empirical attribution
(\textit{Baseline And Per-Phase Throughput Results} subsection of the
\textit{Experimental Setup And Results} section). We can express the
residual quantum-attack
surface after phase $\phi$ as

\begin{equation}
R(\phi) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{5} s_i\,\bigl(1 - c_i(\phi)\bigr),
\label{eq:residual_surface}
\end{equation}

where $s_i>0$ is the severity weight of threat scenario $\mathrm{TS}_i$ and
$c_i(\phi)\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether $\mathrm{TS}_i$ is closed at the end of
phase $\phi$. The migration design drives $R(\phi)$ monotonically to zero:
$R(0)=\sum_i s_i$ (classical baseline), $R(1)>0$ (TS1 remains open),
$R(2)$ closes TS1--TS3, and $R(3)=0$ once the hybrid-TLS layer closes TS4 and the
TLS component of TS5. Figure~\ref{fig:phase_migration_design} presents this
sequence as a timeline; the red Phase~1 badge marks the open TS1 surface that
motivates the multilayer design.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_phase_migration_design.png}
\caption{Three-phase migration sequence.}
\label{fig:phase_migration_design}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Phase 1} activates the PQC BCCSP signing layer only, partially
addressing TS2 and TS3 but leaving TS1 entirely open because the CA's
ECDSA keys remain harvest targets.

\textbf{Phase 2} adds the PQC
certificate infrastructure: all enrolled identities receive post-quantum
certificates, MSP validation uses \textit{buildPQCChain()}, and TS1--TS3
are fully addressed. This is the most operationally significant
step, requiring re-enrolment of all nodes, clients, and administrators
under the \mfabric{} extension.

\textbf{Phase 3} includes the addition of the hybrid TLS key
exchange (\textit{X25519MLKEM768}) for all inter-node connections,
closing TS4 and the TLS component of TS5 and yielding $R(3)=0$.

\subsection{BCCSP LAYER IMPLEMENTATION}
\label{sec:method_bccsp}

The Blockchain Cryptographic Service Provider interface, defined in
\textit{bccsp/bccsp.go}, is Fabric's abstraction for cryptographic
services; no component contains algorithm-specific code outside the
BCCSP. The \mfabric{} plugin is grafted into the software backend
through a self-contained PQC wrapper (\textit{bccsp/sw/pqckey.go}) and a
factory (\textit{bccsp/factory/pqcfactory.go}) that registers the
\textit{PQC} backend without disturbing classical call sites. The plugin
implements the full \textit{bccsp.BCCSP} interface for ML-DSA-44,
ML-DSA-65, and FN-DSA-512, instantiated when \textit{core.yaml} specifies
\textit{Default: PQC}. Key generation invokes CIRCL or
\textit{go-fn-dsa} with \textit{crypto/rand} and stores keys in DER
format. ML-DSA signing uses the pure FIPS~204 mode (full-message with
internal SHAKE-256); FN-DSA signing produces a variable-length
NTRU-lattice signature centred on $\sim$666~bytes, which Fabric's
unbounded protobuf signature fields accommodate without serialisation
changes. \textit{KeyImport} inspects the SPKI OID and either
deserialises post-quantum bytes or delegates classical OIDs to the
software BCCSP; \textit{Hash} and \textit{GetHashOpts} are delegated
unconditionally since SHA-256 and SHA-3 are not replaced.

All Fabric signing paths (peer endorsement, orderer block signing, client
proposal signing) call \textit{Sign} through existing helpers, so
switching \textit{Default} from software to \textit{PQC} redirects all
signing and verification. Algorithm dispatch is table-driven (a map keyed
by algorithm name): adding a new algorithm requires only a new entry, not
new code paths. Private keys are serialised with their algorithm
identifier and type-checked at load to prevent cross-algorithm misuse.
The integration spans approximately 1{,}800 new or modified lines of Go,
distributed across the BCCSP layer (\textit{bccsp/sw/pqckey.go} and
\textit{bccsp/factory/pqcfactory.go}, 296~LOC), the MSP layer
(\textit{msp/pqc.go} validator, 358~LOC, with a 14-LOC dispatch hook
in \textit{msp/mspimpl.go}), nine inter-module TLS delta-lines, the
Fabric CA certificate-issuance path (\textit{fabric-ca/util/pqcsigner.go},
758~LOC), and configuration glue. The external pure-Go
\textit{pqc-bccsp} module provides the algorithm primitives shared by
both the signing backend and the chain validator's dispatch table.

\subsection{CERTIFICATE INFRASTRUCTURE IMPLEMENTATION}
\label{sec:method_ca}

The most significant implementation challenge is the incompatibility between
post-quantum OIDs and Go's \textit{crypto/x509} package, which (through
Go~1.24, the version targeted by this work) supports only RSA and ECDSA
signature algorithms and no post-quantum algorithm. Calling \textit{x509.CreateCertificate} with a
post-quantum key, or \textit{x509.Certificate.Verify} on a post-quantum
signature, returns an unsupported-algorithm error. This barrier is why prior
post-quantum Fabric implementations have relied on offline certificate
generation, leaving the live CA's ECDSA signing key active. This is
precisely the gap that creates the TS1 vulnerability. \mfabric{} resolves the barrier through a
custom certificate construction function in Fabric CA and the custom
\textit{buildPQCChain()} validator in the MSP.

\subsubsection{FABRIC CA POST-QUANTUM EXTENSION}

The \mfabric{} extension adds post-quantum issuance to a single Fabric CA
binary through three coordinated changes. \textit{CreatePQCCertificate}
constructs an X.509 v3 certificate manually in ASN.1 DER per
RFC~5280~\cite{rfc5280}, placing the post-quantum public key under the
NIST OID for ML-DSA-44/65 and the provisional OQS OID
\textit{1.3.9999.3.6} for FN-DSA-512 (to be superseded by the finalised
FIPS~206 OID via a single-constant change). Integration with the signing
path is achieved by registering a hook with the forked CFSSL local
signer at package initialisation: when the certificate template's public
key is a \textit{PQCPublicKey} (including the raw form populated from a
client-submitted PQC CSR), the hook dispatches to
\textit{CreatePQCCertificate} and signs the TBS directly through the
BCCSP plugin with no caller-side pre-hash (ML-DSA and FN-DSA perform the
FIPS-mandated internal hashing). The enrolment handler contains a
complementary fallback: when \textit{x509.ParseCertificateRequest} fails
on an unknown post-quantum OID, \textit{util.ExtractCNFromCSR} extracts
the subject common name directly from the ASN.1 bytes. The resulting
binary remains a drop-in replacement for the standard Fabric CA with
unchanged API and endpoint surface.

The MSP identity certificate is the artefact Fabric authenticates against
in every transaction, endorsement-policy evaluation, and channel
configuration update; the certificate-infrastructure layer is therefore
fully post-quantum end-to-end, from CA root self-signing through runtime
chain validation at the committing peer. The IETF-track
composite-signature construction~\cite{ounsworth2024composite} provides a
future extensibility path that the \mfabric{} dispatch table and
\textit{CreatePQCCertificate} generalise to once it standardises.

\subsection{MSP IDENTITY VALIDATION: THE \textit{buildPQCChain()} VALIDATOR}
\label{sec:method_msp}

MSP identity validation verifies certificate chains for every identity
in channel transactions. The standard MSP uses
\textit{x509.Certificate.Verify}, which rejects post-quantum chains, so
the post-quantum lifecycle requires a dedicated validator.
Figure~\ref{fig:buildpqcchain_flowchart} shows the
\textit{buildPQCChain()} control flow. The validator walks the chain
from end-entity to root under a bounded depth of ten and terminates
successfully when it reaches a self-signed certificate matching a
configured MSP root anchor. At each non-terminal link,
\textit{findIssuerFor} locates the issuer by matching \textit{RawIssuer}
against intermediate-CA and root-CA Subject DNs, then
\textit{verifyChainLink} runs the per-link checks. Algorithm dispatch is
keyed on both the child's \textit{signatureAlgorithm} OID and the
issuer's SPKI OID: classical pairs route to
\textit{Certificate.CheckSignatureFrom}, post-quantum pairs invoke CIRCL
(ML-DSA) or \textit{go-fn-dsa} (FN-DSA) on the
\textit{RawTBSCertificate} with no caller-side pre-hash. A legacy-format
fallback re-resolves dispatch when the child carries a classical
placeholder OID but the issuer's SPKI is post-quantum, and any
cross-algorithm inconsistency is rejected; a SHA-256 pre-hash retry
preserves verifiability of pre-fix-generator credentials. Hybrid chains
mixing classical and post-quantum links are supported to accommodate
phased migrations.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includesvg[inkscapelatex=false,width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_buildpqcchain_flowchart}
\caption{Control flow of \textit{buildPQCChain()}.}
\label{fig:buildpqcchain_flowchart}
\end{figure}

\textit{verifyChainLink} applies five structural checks in order before
signature verification: issuer validity window; child validity window
(skipped at self-signed roots); \textit{BasicConstraints.IsCA};
\textit{keyCertSign} when KeyUsage is set; and AKID/SKID matching when
both are present per RFC~5280~\cite{rfc5280}. The validator is invoked
from \textit{getUniqueValidationChain} whenever a post-quantum
certificate is present; classical-only deployments take the unchanged
Go path. Overhead beyond OID lookup is one post-quantum verification per
identity per block at Op-16.

\subsection{TLS LAYER IMPLEMENTATION}
\label{sec:method_tls}

Go~1.24's \textit{crypto/tls} exposes \textit{X25519MLKEM768} as a
\textit{tls.CurveID} constant, performing both X25519 ECDH and
ML-KEM-768 encapsulation in the ClientHello and deriving the session key

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{sess}} = \mathrm{HKDF}(K_{\mathrm{X25519}}\|K_{\mathrm{MLKEM}}),
\label{eq:hybrid_key}
\end{equation}

which is secure provided either contributing exchange is
secure~\cite{ietf_hybrid_tls, ietf_x25519mlkem768, barker2020transition, bindel2017}.
\mfabric{} sets \textit{CurvePreferences} to
\textit{[tls.X25519MLKEM768, tls.X25519, tls.CurveP256]}, placing the
hybrid first with classical fallbacks; Raft inter-orderer mutual TLS uses
the same configuration. The hybrid exchange closes TS4 and the TLS
component of TS5 independently of the certificate-infrastructure layer.

\section{EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND RESULTS} \label{sec:experimental}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{TEST NETWORK CONFIGURATION}
\label{sec:exp_testnet}

The test network is two organisations (one peer each) and a single Raft
orderer on a single physical host, isolating cryptographic overhead from
network latency. The same \mfabric{} binary is used for all phases:
Phase~1 enables the PQC BCCSP with bootstrap ECDSA certificates from a
modified Cryptogen; Phase~2 adds Fabric~CA with the post-quantum extensions
and re-enrols all identities to post-quantum certificates; Phase~3 adds
\textit{X25519MLKEM768} to the TLS preference list and restarts all nodes.
The standard Fabric asset-transfer-basic chaincode is deployed without
modification. This topology is the smallest deployable network that
exercises the full transaction lifecycle, the endorsement-policy mechanism
(both organisations signing), and the Raft consensus path, while keeping
the cryptographic signal uncontaminated by WAN latency, since loopback
transport fully exercises ML-KEM-768 encapsulation and decapsulation. The resulting
throughput is an upper bound for the same configuration on a distributed
network.

\subsection{BENCHMARK METHODOLOGY}
\label{sec:exp_benchmethod}

The benchmark runs on a single host (Intel Core i9-9980HK, 8 cores at
2.4--5.0~GHz, 64~GB DDR4, macOS, Docker) executing Hyperledger Fabric
v2.5.12 (commit \textit{af0b647}), Fabric CA v1.5.12, Hyperledger
Caliper v0.6.0, Go~1.24, Cloudflare CIRCL v1.6.3, and \textit{go-fn-dsa}
v0.2.0. Channel configuration uses a 500-transaction block, 2-second
timeout, and any-1-of-2 endorsement policy executing the standard
asset-transfer chaincode. Caliper~\cite{caliper2023} sweeps thirteen
send rates (25--1{,}000~TPS) for three independent runs in both
write-heavy (90\%) and mixed (50\%) profiles, with a clean genesis
reset before each write-heavy run.

Maximum sustainable throughput is identified as the plateau-region mean

\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{\max} \;=\; |\mathcal{P}|^{-1}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{ach}}(\rho),
\quad
\mathcal{P}=\{\rho:|d\Gamma_{\mathrm{ach}}/d\rho|\le\varepsilon\},
\label{eq:plateau}
\end{equation}

with $\Gamma_{\mathrm{ach}}(\rho)$ the achieved throughput at send rate
$\rho$ and $\varepsilon$ the plateau tolerance. Four metrics are
reported: $\Gamma_{\max}$ (TPS), saturation latency, ledger storage
(MB/peer, measured directly after each run), and block propagation rate
(blocks/s from Prometheus over a 30-minute window).

\subsection{VERIFICATION AND CORRECTNESS ASSURANCE}
\label{sec:exp_verify}

ML-DSA primitive correctness inherits from Cloudflare CIRCL's upstream
NIST ACVP coverage for FIPS~204; FN-DSA correctness is verified through
round-trip integration tests on real X.509 \textit{TBSCertificate}
fixtures since ACVP vectors for \textit{go-fn-dsa} v0.2.0 are not yet
available. The plugin satisfies the
\textit{Sign}/\textit{Verify}/\textit{KeyImport}/\textit{GetKey} contracts
through unmodified call sites in peer, orderer, and client SDK; the
table-driven test suite sweeps all three PQ algorithms and confirms
error-path semantics (tampered signatures, expired certificates, unknown
issuers, untrusted self-signed). End-to-end execution covers the five
lifecycle phases in each of the three deployment phases for all three
algorithms with zero failures. With $n=27$ benchmark runs per workload
and $\approx 1.7\times 10^{5}$ committed post-quantum transactions, the
Clopper-Pearson 95\% upper bound on the per-transaction failure
probability is $p<1.8\times 10^{-5}$.

Four quantitative properties are established: (i) integration coverage

\begin{equation}
\delta_{\mfabric} =
\frac{|\{\text{post-quantum deployed}\}|}
{|\{\text{ECDLP-vulnerable identified}\}|}
= \frac{4}{4} = 1,
\label{eq:integration_coverage}
\end{equation}

with no prior published integration achieving
$\delta=1$~\cite{holcomb2021, hu2025prototype, abbas2025};
(ii) signature-size amplification of 9.3$\times$, 33.6$\times$, and
46.0$\times$ for FN-DSA-512, ML-DSA-44, and ML-DSA-65 relative to 72-byte
ECDSA; (iii) hybrid TLS session-key security
from~(\ref{eq:hybrid_key}), secure as long as either contributing
exchange is secure; and (iv) zero verification failures across all ACVP,
round-trip, interface, and end-to-end tests.

\subsection{BASELINE AND PER-PHASE THROUGHPUT RESULTS}
\label{sec:exp_results}

\subsubsection{ECDSA P-256 BASELINE}

The ECDSA P-256 baseline achieves \textbf{275.3~TPS} (standard deviation
1.35) write-heavy with 0.18-s plateau latency and 1{,}690~MB per-peer
ledger, and \textbf{344.2~TPS} (standard deviation 1.93) mixed with
0.11-s latency and 3{,}266~MB per-peer ledger. The saturation knee sits
at 200--300~TPS write-heavy and 300--400~TPS mixed. These figures are
consistent with prior characterisations of standard
Fabric~\cite{androulaki2018, baliga2018performance, kuzlu2021performance, nasir2018}
after accounting for hardware and topology differences.

\subsubsection{PHASE 1: BCCSP POST-QUANTUM SIGNING}

Phase~1 replaces ECDSA signing at the BCCSP layer while leaving
certificates and TLS unchanged, isolating the signature-algorithm impact.
All three algorithms follow the same sub-knee saturation profile as ECDSA;
above the knee each plateau sits below the ECDSA ceiling in proportion to
signature size, with FN-DSA-512 incurring the smallest reduction
($-1.7\%$ both workloads), ML-DSA-44 next ($-4.2\%$ write, $-1.4\%$
mixed), and ML-DSA-65 close behind ($-3.5\%$ write, $-4.1\%$ mixed). The
write-heavy ML-DSA-65/ML-DSA-44 gap is within ML-DSA-65's standard
deviation (6.55~TPS, the study's largest). Plateau latencies are
0.18--0.20~s, indistinguishable from baseline. Per-phase numbers are
consolidated in Table~\ref{tab:phase_attribution}.

\subsubsection{PHASE 2: POST-QUANTUM CERTIFICATE INFRASTRUCTURE}

Phase~2 adds post-quantum X.509 infrastructure: the CA issues
post-quantum-signed certificates and \textit{buildPQCChain()} validates
them at Op-16 (the committing-peer chain re-validation, run for every
endorsement signature in every block). The certificate layer introduces
substantially larger reductions than Phase~1 for the ML-DSA family:
FN-DSA-512 declines by 3.3\%/3.2\% (write/mixed) from the 1.7\%
Phase~1 penalty, reflecting per-endorsement SPKI cost; ML-DSA-44 by
9.9\%/8.9\%; and ML-DSA-65 by 18.1\%/13.0\%, the largest single-phase
impact measured.

The dominant Phase~2 effect is not throughput reduction but
certificate-processing latency (Figure~\ref{fig:latency_phase}). Phase~1
latencies are indistinguishable from the 0.18-s ECDSA baseline; the
Phase~2 transition produces a qualitative jump to 1.71~s for FN-DSA-512
(9.5$\times$), 5.47~s for ML-DSA-44 (30$\times$), and 10.20~s for
ML-DSA-65 (57$\times$). This reflects the Op-16
bottleneck~\cite{thakkar2018}: a 500-transaction write-heavy block carries
up to 500 chain validations, each over an SPKI of 897, 1{,}312, or
1{,}952~bytes respectively (versus 65 for ECDSA). Mixed-workload latencies
are far lower (0.14, 0.18, 0.77~s) because fewer write transactions per
unit time mean fewer certificates per block.

\subsubsection{PHASE 3: COMPLETE \texorpdfstring{M\textsuperscript{2}FABRIC}{M2FABRIC} DEPLOYMENT}

Phase~3 activates hybrid TLS (\textit{X25519MLKEM768}) for all
inter-node connections, completing the deployment. FN-DSA-512 achieves
265.5~TPS write-heavy and 332.7~TPS mixed ($-3.5\%$, $-3.3\%$);
ML-DSA-44 achieves 245.2 and 317.0~TPS ($-10.9\%$, $-7.9\%$); and
ML-DSA-65 achieves 225.8 and 301.1~TPS ($-18.0\%$, $-12.5\%$).
Phase~2-to-Phase~3 changes are small, span zero, and lie within each
Phase~3 standard-deviation envelope: hybrid TLS contributes a
per-transaction overhead below the detection threshold of the $n=3$
design, as expected when connection-level key-exchange cost amortises
across many transactions per TLS session.

\subsubsection{THROUGHPUT DEGRADATION, LATENCY, AND STORAGE MODELS}

Throughput means are reported with 95\% Student-$t$ intervals
($t_{0.025,2}=4.303$); adjacent-algorithm intervals are non-overlapping
for the headline write-heavy comparisons. The ordering FN-DSA-512 $>$
ML-DSA-44 $>$ ML-DSA-65 is robust to a non-parametric bootstrap
($10^4$ resamples) and to very large effect sizes (Cohen's
$d\approx 18, 12$). Only small-effect comparisons (notably hybrid TLS)
are underpowered; $n\geq 5$ with ANOVA is recommended for those.
Phase~2 write latencies follow from the Op-16 certificate-validation
bottleneck: empirically $\tau_{\mathrm{cert}}\propto|\mathit{pk}|^{\beta}$
with $\beta\approx 2.30$, so doubling the public-key size quintuples
per-certificate validation time.

Ledger storage (Table~\ref{tab:storage_results}) confirms signature
size as a second-order contributor: all three post-quantum algorithms
produce write-heavy ledgers within 4\% of the 1{,}690~MB ECDSA
baseline, with per-write on-disk overhead of 1{,}706--1{,}756~bytes
across all four algorithms despite signatures ranging from 72 to
3{,}309 bytes. The mechanism is Fabric's block-level identity model:
endorsement certificates are stored by reference in the peer identity
store rather than embedded in every block, so signature size affects
transmission and verification cost but not ledger footprint. For the
mixed workload the physically meaningful metric is per-run ledger
growth (the increment added by one benchmark run), which isolates a
single run's contribution independently of the network's prior
state; the three post-quantum values agree to within two percent and
the 0.74--0.75$\times$ multipliers relative to the ECDSA baseline
quantify a throughput-mediated reduction (fewer committed
transactions per unit time), not a per-transaction storage saving.

\subsection{ALGORITHM SELECTION GUIDANCE}
\label{sec:res_selection}

The phase attribution confirms the cost-model prediction
(Eq.~\ref{eq:selection_cost}): per-endorsement payload size, not signing
speed, is the dominant determinant. FN-DSA-512 delivers the highest
end-to-end throughput despite signing $\sim$53\% more slowly than
ML-DSA-44 in microbenchmarks, because its smaller signature and public
key give a far smaller per-endorsement payload. Phase~3 deployment
guidance follows from the throughput--latency trade-off:
\emph{FN-DSA-512} (Category 1, $-3.5\%/-3.3\%$ write/mixed,
$10\times$ latency) is the primary choice for throughput-sensitive
deployments and early-transition pilots; \emph{ML-DSA-44} (Category 2,
$-10.9\%/-7.9\%$, $30\times$ latency) is the balanced option and NIST's
first-choice signature; \emph{ML-DSA-65} (Category 3, $-18.0\%/-12.5\%$,
$58\times$ latency) is reserved for high-assurance deployments where
Category 3 is regulatorily mandated.

\subsection{SECURITY ANALYSIS OF THE IMPLEMENTATION}
\label{sec:res_security}

The security rests on three foundations: the security of the post-quantum
algorithms (established by NIST's six-year standardisation
process~\cite{alagic2022nist}; no polynomial-time quantum algorithm is known for
the underlying Module LWE or NTRU/SIS problems), the correctness of the
implementation (established by the verification reported in the
\textit{Verification And Correctness Assurance} subsection of the
\textit{Experimental Setup And Results} section), and the integrity of
the integration design (which preserves the existing Fabric
security model: the BCCSP contract is satisfied exactly, MSP policy evaluation is
unchanged, the TLS configuration is a subset of Go's option space, and CA-issued
certificates follow the RFC~5280 structure).

The custom code paths added above the primitive layer are not covered by upstream
review and warrant explicit identification of residual attack surface. Four
classes of concern apply. \textit{Malformed-certificate handling}: the custom
ASN.1 parser in \textit{buildPQCChain()} operates on attacker-controlled input;
it rejects parsing failures by error-return rather than panic, but
positive evidence of robustness requires coverage-guided fuzz testing, which
is not yet conducted. \textit{Algorithm-OID spoofing}: the dispatch table checks
issuer-SPKI/child consistency, so a substituted classical-for-post-quantum
signature fails; however, certificates presenting a known PQC OID with
wrong-length key bytes have not been tested under adversarial corpora.
\textit{Downgrade resistance}: the legacy SHA-256-prehash fallback introduces a
second acceptance path; a strict-mode toggle disabling it after a cutover date is
recommended for production. \textit{Out-of-scope coverage}: the
post-quantum certificate-infrastructure layer of \mfabric{} is scoped to
Fabric's MSP identity model; transport-layer endpoint infrastructure is
outside the Fabric cryptographic security model and is not part of the
contribution.

A four-stage adversarial evaluation is scheduled as immediate follow-up:
\textit{go-fuzz} coverage-guided fuzzing of the
\textit{buildPQCChain()} ASN.1 entry points; an OID-spoofing corpus
exercising truncated/oversized SPKI payloads, cross-algorithm
OID/key mismatches, and AKID/SKID-disjoint chains; downgrade-resistance
testing with the legacy SHA-256-prehash fallback disabled; and
constant-time differential analysis on the signing and verification
paths to detect regressions in the upstream CIRCL and \textit{go-fn-dsa}
constant-time guarantees. The implementation's security stance is
therefore \textit{provisional} pending this workstream and the
limitations consolidated in \S\ref{sec:disc_limits}.

%=====================================================================
\section{DISCUSSION} \label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================

This section consolidates the implications of the results of
Section~\ref{sec:experimental} along four dimensions: the completeness of
the multilayer approach relative to prior partial integrations, the
amplification of the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat by ledger
immutability, the generalisability of the architecture to other permissioned
blockchain platforms, and the alignment of the design with emerging national
and international post-quantum migration strategies.

\subsection{COMPLETENESS OF THE MULTILAYER APPROACH}
\label{sec:disc_completeness}

The integration-coverage identity~(\ref{eq:integration_coverage}) gives
$\delta_{\mfabric{}}=4/4=1$ with respect to the four ECDLP-vulnerable
layers. Any integration that leaves a strict subset classical maintains
a strictly positive residual surface
$R(\phi)$~(\ref{eq:residual_surface}); a BCCSP-only integration (the
shape of several prior proposals) leaves TS1 entirely open because a
quantum adversary who recovers the classical CA's private key can issue
fraudulent \emph{post-quantum} certificates that the post-quantum MSP
accepts. The empirical phase progression in
Table~\ref{tab:phase_attribution} confirms each successive phase
contributes a measurable but bounded throughput cost: the coverage gain
is not paid for by performance collapse, and \emph{partial migration is
a security anti-pattern, not a transitional state} since Phase 1 alone
provides no protection against the highest-severity threat (TS1) while
paying most of the throughput cost. The per-endorsement-payload finding
extends the PQFabric~\cite{holcomb2021} observation to the
FIPS-finalised algorithm generation, and the pure-Go choice distinguishes
\mfabric{} from CGo-bound prior work~\cite{stebila2016oqs}.

A second implication is that permissioned blockchains amplify the
harvest-now-decrypt-later threat structurally: the append-only ledger
guarantees that the harvest set $\mathcal{H}(t)$ is non-decreasing, so
every ECDSA-signed transaction, endorsement, block header, and
certificate committed before migration remains a permanent harvest
target. The coverage identity~(\ref{eq:integration_coverage}) addresses
\emph{future} cryptographic material only; at the 275.3~TPS write-heavy
plateau, each day of continued ECDSA operation accumulates
$\sim 2.4\times 10^{7}$ key-equivalents into $\mathcal{H}$, so migration
urgency is proportional to both the residual security lifetime $Z$ in
Mosca's inequality~\cite{mosca2018} and the rate at which the unprotected
ledger continues to accumulate harvestable material.

\subsection{GENERALISABILITY TO OTHER PERMISSIONED BLOCKCHAINS}
\label{sec:disc_general}

The four-layer decomposition is not Fabric-specific: the same
operational categories recur in Quorum, Besu, Corda, and most
enterprise-grade permissioned platforms. The most transferable artefact
is the \textit{buildPQCChain()} pattern: any Go-based blockchain that
imports \textit{crypto/x509} is currently subject to the same
post-quantum OID rejection, and the same
dispatch-on-\textit{signatureAlgorithm} structure resolves it. Two
qualifications apply: FN-DSA-512's OID is provisionally assigned within
the OQS experimental arc \textit{1.3.9999.3.x} until FIPS~206 is
finalised (requiring a transition-window dispatch in any portable
validator), and hybrid TLS support outside Go~1.24+ requires either
analogous stdlib support or in-process key-exchange code.

The design also aligns with three concurrently developing migration
frameworks: the NIST transition roadmap~\cite{nist_ir8547} (RSA/ECC
deprecation by 2030, disallowance by 2035), the NSA Commercial National
Security Algorithm Suite~2.0~\cite{nsa_cnsa2} (full adoption by 2035),
and emerging national strategies such as the draft Brunei Post-Quantum
Cryptography Migration Framework~\cite{csb_brunei2026}.
\mfabric{}'s three-phase migration sequences these frameworks' Validate
and Deploy stages for the Fabric platform specifically, supplying a
concrete \emph{how} for the permissioned-blockchain layer of a national
migration whose policy frameworks otherwise specify only the \emph{what}
and \emph{when}.

\subsection{LIMITATIONS AND THREATS TO VALIDITY}
\label{sec:disc_limits}

Four limitations bound the results.
\emph{(i)~Single-host benchmark with $n=3$}: the topology isolates
cryptographic overhead from network latency; multi-host deployments will
be additionally bounded by gossip-propagation costs scaling with
signature size, and a multi-host re-run with $n\geq 5$ and ANOVA is
required to characterise that regime and to detect small effects
(notably hybrid TLS) that the present design is underpowered for.
\emph{(ii)~Functional rather than adversarial verification}: the
fuzz-testing, adversarial-corpus, and downgrade-resistance gaps are
addressed by the planned four-stage workstream
(\S\ref{sec:res_security}). \emph{(iii)~Provisional FN-DSA-512 OID}:
the transition to the finalised FIPS~206 OID is a single-constant
change but requires a transition-window dispatch entry.
\emph{(iv)~Side-channel scope}: primitive-level constant-time guarantees
inherit from CIRCL and \textit{go-fn-dsa}; HSM deployment and
EM/fault-injection characterisation~\cite{ravi2024sidechannel} remain
open. External validity is bounded by the single chaincode workload;
construct validity rests on the plateau-mean
operationalisation~(\ref{eq:plateau}).

\section{CONCLUSION} \label{sec:conclusion}
%=====================================================================

This article presented \mfabric{}, the first published runtime
integration replacing all four live cryptographic layers of the current
Hyperledger Fabric LTS with FIPS-standardised post-quantum algorithms in
unmodified Go: BCCSP signing (ML-DSA-44/65, FN-DSA-512), MSP certificate
infrastructure (post-quantum Fabric CA with custom ASN.1 DER
construction), MSP identity validation (the \textit{buildPQCChain()}
validator), and TLS transport (\textit{X25519MLKEM768} hybrid key
exchange). The three-phase migration drives the residual quantum-attack
surface monotonically to zero. Empirically, $n=27$ Caliper runs per
workload show the complete Phase~3 deployment reduces maximum
sustainable write-heavy throughput by 3.5\%, 10.9\%, and 18.0\% for
FN-DSA-512, ML-DSA-44, and ML-DSA-65 relative to the 275.3~TPS ECDSA
baseline; per-endorsement payload size, not signing speed, dominates,
and the certificate layer is the dominant cost driver while hybrid TLS
is effectively free per-transaction.

Three implications follow: partial migration is a security anti-pattern
(an operator deploying only the signing layer pays most of the
throughput cost for no protection against TS1); the
dispatch-on-\textit{signatureAlgorithm} chain-validation pattern
transfers directly to any Go-based permissioned blockchain that imports
\textit{crypto/x509}; and the design aligns with the deprecation
timelines of NIST~IR~8547, the NSA CNSA~2.0 suite, and emerging national
migration frameworks. The follow-up agenda comprises a multi-host
benchmark at $n\geq 5$ with ANOVA, the four-stage adversarial-evaluation
workstream, HQC integration as a diversity backup KEM once
FIPS-standardised, and HSM characterisation.
